The metal floor beneath Ines’ boots vibrated like a whale’s ribcage in a tide. She steadied herself on the spiral ladder, the lantern at the summit casting a pale circle over the rust‑spattered glass. Around the island, the dust‑laden sky stretched forever, a muted veil that turned day into a permanent dusk. The lighthouse, a monolith built on seabed, hummed with the pulse of its array, its lenses turning like a lantern fish’s bioluminescent lure, searching the indifferent horizon.

Ines, seventy‑two, had been an oceanographer before the seas grew silent. Her hands, calloused from years of handling trawling nets, now tended the generator, the radio, the weathered brass of the signal lamp. The solitude pressed against her ribs as the ocean’s pressure once had pressed against a submersible hull.

A sharp click burst through the static, a series of short and long pulses that resolved into a pattern she recognized from old maritime training. The code spelled a name: Marinara—the cargo ship that vanished in the Great Dust Storm of 2107, lost with its crew forty years prior. The signal repeated, relentless, as if the vessel itself were trying to cut through the suffocating haze.

She recalled the night the sea had swallowed the research vessel, and the promise she made to watch over the waters. The memory lingered, a tide that never receded, and it was why she had accepted this lonely watch.

Ines lifted the antique telegraph key, her fingers trembling like jellyfish tentacles in a current, and sent a reply: “— ···— …”. The reply drifted back into the void, a string of dots and dashes that dissolved into dust‑filled air. Minutes stretched; the lighthouse’s rotating lens painted circles on the concrete floor, each rotation a reminder of time’s relentless march.

She imagined the Marinara’s hull, now a rusted skeleton beneath waves that no longer rose, its crew frozen in a moment of panic, their cries swallowed by twilight. The signal seemed to come from a place beyond coordinates, a resonance that defied the maps stored in the lighthouse’s memory banks. Her mind, trained to chart currents, now tried to chart a phantom frequency.

Outside, a distant gull—an engineered species adapted to the dim sky—glided past the island’s edge, its wings a silver flash against the amber haze. Ines watched it, feeling the familiar tug of longing that had once driven her to chase plankton blooms across abyssal plains. The lighthouse’s light turned, its beam cutting through the dust like a shark’s tooth slicing a school of sardines, yet the darkness remained stubborn.

She turned the key again, this time sending a longer series of pulses, hoping to coax more words from the dead ship. The radio crackled, then fell silent, leaving only soft hum of the generator and faint thrum of the sea below the island’s foundation. In the stillness, a single thought rose, weightless as a plankton bloom drifting in a dead current.

A thin filament of light slipped through a cracked pane, stretching toward endless gray, as if reaching for something unseen.
